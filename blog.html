Trafy launches world's first "Learn by Building" cohort for AI engineers

Trafy has opened applications for Cohort '26, a six-month, fully online program built entirely around one idea: the fastest way to learn to build with AI is to actually build something, with founders, for real users, under a real deadline.

Unlike a bootcamp or a lecture series, there's no fixed curriculum to sit through. Each cohort starts with a shared foundation in AI product engineering, then splits into two tracks: one for builders chasing a startup idea, one for builders aiming to get hired. Both groups spend the bulk of their six months shipping, not studying.

"The cohort is designed to help founders, students and business leaders master AI engineering through hands-on building, real-world projects and community-driven learning," said a Trafy spokesperson at the program's public launch. Cohort '25 graduates have gone on to raise grant funding, sit in VC meetings and land offers at international companies. Trafy is aiming to repeat, and beat, those outcomes with Cohort '26.

Applications for Cohort '26 are open now, with seats going fast.

Trafy becomes the first company in the world to introduce an Employability Index

Trafy has introduced the Employability Index, a single, verifiable score that measures how ready a candidate actually is to contribute from day one, making it the first company anywhere to formalise hiring readiness into one composite number instead of a resume, a college name and a gut-feel interview.

The score is built from three signals that, until now, no one had combined into a single pipeline: a 45-question round covering core computer science fundamentals, two Data Structures & Algorithms problems solved live against a 45-minute clock, and a cross-check against a candidate's portfolio, LinkedIn, LeetCode and GitHub. Each layer catches something the others can't, breadth of knowledge, applied problem-solving under pressure, and a real, sustained track record.

"Hiring has run on proxies for too long, a keyword match, a brand name, an hour of small talk," said a Trafy spokesperson. "The Employability Index replaces the proxy with a verified signal. Companies get a faster, fairer way to screen. Candidates get one score that actually reflects what they can do."

The Employability Index now runs as part of the Employability Path inside every Trafy Cohort, and early partner companies are already using it to shortlist Cohort '25 graduates directly, no resume screening required.

Inside Week 3: how five teams shipped MVPs in four days

By the start of Week 3, every team in Cohort '25 had a validated problem and a rough plan. What they didn't have yet was a product anyone could actually use, and they had four days to fix that.

Monday morning opened with a single rule from the mentors on the floor: ship something a stranger could click through by Thursday, even if it's held together with duct tape. No team spent more than a day on the "perfect" tech stack decision. Most defaulted to whatever let them move fastest, then iterated in public as feedback came in from the rest of the cohort.

What stood out wasn't the tooling, it was the rhythm: stand-ups at 9am, user tests in the afternoon, and a demo, however rough, at the end of every single day. By Thursday night, five teams had working MVPs on stage, three of them with a handful of real signups already.

"You build, you get feedback, you ship, you grow," is how one Cohort '25 builder put it afterward. It's become something of an unofficial motto for the program.

Journal Apr 18, 2026 · 5 min read

From prototype to funded startup: what Cohort '25 taught us about traction

When Cohort '25 wrapped, the numbers surprised even the mentors who'd been in the room every week: over ₹1 Cr in grants allocated to student startups, 25+ VC meetings booked for selected teams, and seven startups that went straight from cohort project to incubation.

None of that happened because the ideas were flawless on day one. Most weren't. It happened because every team was forced to find real users before they were allowed to obsess over the product. Pitch Day wasn't the finish line; for most teams it was the first time they said their idea out loud to someone who could actually write a check.

The pattern that separated the funded startups from the rest wasn't polish. It was evidence. Teams that walked into investor meetings with even a handful of active users and a clear "here's what changed after we shipped this" story consistently out-performed teams with slicker decks and no traction to back them up.

It's the same principle Cohort '26 is being built around: build first, pitch second, and let the traction do the talking.
